# Zero-Distribution Source Notes

## Product-native distribution

Dropbox’s current referral design makes the offered value itself the incentive: each successful account creation and validation adds storage to both inviter and invitee. The product also permits an email invitation or a reusable invitation link. Its S-1 describes viral, bottom-up workplace adoption and a global sharing network as central growth mechanisms, while noting that individual users brought the product into offices. **Transferable implication:** reward the completed value-creating interaction, not raw invitations; make a shareable object useful to the recipient before they become a user. [1] [2]

Calendly describes a similarly product-native acquisition surface: users copy an availability link, embed it, use integrations, or include it in an invitation. A recipient interacts with a simple booking page rather than an ad. **Transferable implication:** a Referrer’s “open to referral requests” link or an employee-shared role card can serve a comparable recipient-first introduction, provided visibility is opt-in and privacy-preserving. [3]

## Community, creator, and template ecosystems

Notion’s Marketplace shows a large catalogue of community templates, creator profiles, consulting partners, and categories. Canva’s templates directory exposes many high-intent use cases, including resumes. These sources demonstrate durable distribution surfaces built around reusable work rather than generic social posting. **Transferable implication:** skipwait.me can publish useful, searchable, role-specific assets—such as employee referral-request checklists, role-link explainers, and application trackers—without publishing private job seeker documents or inventing openings. [4] [5]

## Bottom-up team adoption and concentrated marketplace liquidity

Slack’s S-1 says its growth was largely driven by word-of-mouth and that organizational usage typically began bottoms-up with end users. The founder’s launch account describes recruiting a small number of companies, expanding gradually to larger groups, and treating the product’s meaningful shared-use threshold as a key retention signal. **Transferable implication:** begin with a narrow, observable cohort—such as a handful of companies and job families—then expand after both sides demonstrate repeat use. [6] [7]

The two-sided-platform literature defines these businesses as facilitating value-creating interactions across distinct customer groups and explains why asymmetric pricing can be economically rational. Marketplace practice emphasizes liquidity, supply-side support, curation, clear expectations, and trust mechanisms. **Transferable implication:** Referrers are constrained supply, so their safety, control, response effort, and reputation must be prioritised before broad Job Seeker acquisition. [8] [9]

## Focused-network seeding

In a 2009 Startup School interview, Mark Zuckerberg described Facebook’s organic early campus spread, deliberate launches at schools where demand was evident, and later use of friend-import tools only where they fit the product. The enduring lesson is not to copy contact import: it is to begin inside a bounded social graph with a clear shared context, prove that the product is valuable there, and expand deliberately. **Transferable implication:** skipwait.me should begin with explicit company-and-role clusters, opt-in invitations, and useful shared context—not address-book scraping or unconsented outreach. [10]
